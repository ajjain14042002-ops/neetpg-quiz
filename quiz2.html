Question 1 of 40Surgery High-yield concept
After an episode of forceful vomiting following a heavy meal and alcohol, a man develops severe chest pain and subcutaneous emphysema in the neck. The MOST common site of oesophageal rupture is:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: C. Left posterolateral wall of the lower thoracic oesophagus

Explanation

This is Boerhaave syndrome (spontaneous full-thickness rupture), shown by Mackler's triad: vomiting, chest pain and subcutaneous emphysema. Correct: the tear is usually in the LEFT posterolateral wall of the distal oesophagus, 2–3 cm above the GE junction, where the wall is weakest and has no supporting structures. That is why a LEFT pleural effusion or pneumothorax follows. Diagnose with a water-soluble contrast swallow or CT. Why the others are wrong: the mid-oesophagus is not the usual site. Killian's dehiscence (cervical) is the commonest site of IATROGENIC (endoscopic) perforation. The abdominal oesophagus is not the typical site. A Mallory–Weiss tear is only a MUCOSAL tear, near the GE junction.

One-liner to remember: Boerhaave = full-thickness tear, left posterolateral distal oesophagus; iatrogenic perforation = cervical (Killian's).
Concept: Boerhaave syndrome – site of rupture

Question 2 of 40OBG High-yield concept
A woman with carcinoma cervix has para-aortic lymph node metastasis confirmed on imaging, with no other distant spread. Her FIGO (2018) stage is:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: D. IIIC2

Explanation

FIGO 2018 added lymph-node status to staging. Correct: para-aortic node involvement = IIIC2 (with an 'r' for radiology or 'p' for pathology). Why the others are wrong: IIIC1 = PELVIC lymph nodes only. IVA = spread to the mucosa of the bladder or rectum. IVB = distant metastasis (for example lung, liver, bone, or inguinal and supraclavicular nodes). Para-aortic nodes are regional, so the stage is IIIC2, not IVB – a commonly forgotten update.

One-liner to remember: FIGO 2018 cervix: pelvic nodes IIIC1, para-aortic IIIC2, bladder/rectal mucosa IVA, distant IVB.
Concept: FIGO 2018 staging of carcinoma cervix
Question 3 of 40Surgery High-yield concept
A patient recovers from MILD acute gallstone pancreatitis. The recommended timing of cholecystectomy is:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: B. During the same admission, once the pancreatitis has settled

Explanation

Correct: after MILD gallstone pancreatitis, laparoscopic cholecystectomy should be done during the SAME admission (or within about 2 weeks). Delaying it leads to recurrent pancreatitis, cholangitis or cholecystitis in up to 25–30% of patients. Why the others are wrong: delaying 6–8 weeks is for SEVERE pancreatitis with peripancreatic collections or necrosis. Waiting for a second attack is unsafe. Very early surgery in the acute phase is not recommended; urgent ERCP within 24–72 h is reserved for co-existing CHOLANGITIS or persistent biliary obstruction.

One-liner to remember: Mild biliary pancreatitis → cholecystectomy same admission; severe with collections → delay ~6 weeks; ERCP only for cholangitis/obstruction.
Concept: Gallstone pancreatitis – timing of cholecystectomy
Question 4 of 40PSM / Community Medicine High-yield concept
The incidence of lung cancer is 40 per 1000 among smokers and 10 per 1000 among non-smokers. The attributable risk (as a percentage) is:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: A. 75%

Explanation

Attributable risk % = (incidence in exposed − incidence in non-exposed) ÷ incidence in exposed × 100 = (40 − 10) ÷ 40 × 100 = 75%. This means 75% of lung cancer among smokers is due to smoking and could be prevented by stopping. Why the others are wrong: '30%' confuses the absolute risk difference (30 per 1000) with a percentage. 25% is the non-exposed incidence as a fraction of the exposed (10 ÷ 40). 4 is the RELATIVE RISK (40 ÷ 10), which measures the strength of association, not attributable risk.

One-liner to remember: RR = Ie/Iu (strength of association); AR% = (Ie − Iu)/Ie × 100 (preventable fraction).
Concept: Attributable risk (percent)
Question 5 of 40OBG High-yield concept
A Rh-negative, unsensitised mother delivers a Rh-positive baby. A standard 300 µg dose of anti-D immunoglobulin protects against fetomaternal haemorrhage of up to:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: B. 30 mL of fetal whole blood (about 15 mL fetal red cells)

Explanation

Correct: 300 µg anti-D neutralises about 30 mL of fetal WHOLE blood, equal to roughly 15 mL of fetal RED CELLS (Williams). Give it within 72 hours of delivery. If the Kleihauer–Betke test (or flow cytometry) shows a larger bleed, give an extra dose for each additional 30 mL. Why the others are wrong: '15 mL' confuses whole blood with packed red cells – the classic trap. 5 mL is too low, and 300 mL is a confusion with the µg dose figure. In the first trimester 50 µg is enough because the fetal blood volume is small.

One-liner to remember: 300 µg anti-D = 30 mL fetal whole blood = 15 mL fetal RBCs; give within 72 h.
Concept: Anti-D immunoglobulin dosing
Question 6 of 40Pediatrics Image-based
IMAGE-BASED: A 3-year-old has had fever, cough, coryza and red watery eyes for 3 days. The buccal mucosa opposite the lower molars is shown. Which statement about this illness is CORRECT?
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: D. The child is infectious from about 4 days before to 4 days after the rash appears

Explanation

The image shows Koplik spots (tiny grey-white 'grains of salt' on a red base) – pathognomonic of measles in the prodrome. Correct: measles is infectious from ~4 days before to ~4 days after rash onset (Park), so isolate till day 4 of rash. Why the others are wrong: Koplik spots appear 1–2 days BEFORE the rash and fade within 2–3 days after it appears – they are not a late sign. The commonest cause of death is PNEUMONIA (viral or secondary bacterial); SSPE is rare and appears 7–10 years later. Vitamin A is given to EVERY child with measles (WHO: 2 doses 24 h apart, age-appropriate dose; a 3rd dose a few weeks later if there are eye signs) because it cuts mortality and blindness.

One-liner to remember: Koplik spots precede rash; infectious 4 days before → 4 days after rash; death = pneumonia; vitamin A to ALL measles cases.
Concept: Measles – infectivity, Koplik spots, vitamin A

Question 7 of 40Medicine High-yield concept
On day 7 of unfractionated heparin, a patient's platelet count falls by 55% and a new DVT develops. The most appropriate next step is:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: D. Stop all heparin and start a non-heparin anticoagulant (e.g. argatroban or fondaparinux)

Explanation

This is type II HIT: IgG antibodies against the heparin–PF4 complex activate platelets, causing thrombocytopenia with paradoxical THROMBOSIS, typically 5–10 days after starting heparin (4T score). Correct: stop ALL heparin (including flushes) and start a non-heparin anticoagulant – argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux or a DOAC. Why the others are wrong: LMWH cross-reacts with the antibodies. Starting warfarin acutely lowers protein C quickly and can cause venous limb gangrene or skin necrosis – start it only once platelets recover (≥150 × 10⁹/L), overlapping with another agent. Platelet transfusion can fuel thrombosis and is avoided unless there is bleeding.

One-liner to remember: HIT = thrombosis, not bleeding: stop all heparin, give argatroban/fondaparinux; no LMWH, no early warfarin, no platelets.
Concept: Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia – management
Question 8 of 40Psychiatry High-yield concept
A man admitted for surgery had his last drink of alcohol on Monday morning. Delirium tremens is most likely to begin:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: C. 48–72 hours after the last drink

Explanation

Know the withdrawal timeline. Correct: delirium tremens (confusion, coarse tremor, visual hallucinations, autonomic hyperactivity) usually starts 48–72 hours after the last drink, peaks around day 3 and lasts 3–5 days; untreated mortality can reach 5–15%. Why the others are wrong: 6–12 h is minor withdrawal (tremor, anxiety, sweating). 12–48 h is when withdrawal SEIZURES (rum fits) and alcoholic hallucinosis (auditory hallucinations with a clear sensorium) occur. Symptoms starting after 7–10 days suggest another cause. Treatment: benzodiazepines (chlordiazepoxide, or lorazepam/oxazepam if the liver is diseased) plus thiamine BEFORE glucose.

One-liner to remember: Withdrawal: tremor 6–12 h → seizures/hallucinosis 12–48 h → DT 48–72 h.
Concept: Alcohol withdrawal timeline
Question 9 of 40Microbiology High-yield concept
Which of the following is NOT reliably destroyed by standard autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 minutes?
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: A. Prions

Explanation

Correct: prions are highly resistant to heat, formalin, radiation and UV. They need extended autoclaving (134 °C for at least 18 minutes, porous load) or 1N NaOH / sodium hypochlorite treatment followed by autoclaving, and instruments are often discarded. Why the others are wrong: Geobacillus (Bacillus) stearothermophilus spores are among the most heat-resistant spores, which is exactly why they are the BIOLOGICAL INDICATOR for autoclaves – a proper cycle kills them. HBV and Clostridium tetani spores are also killed by a standard cycle.

One-liner to remember: Autoclave indicator = Geobacillus stearothermophilus; prions need 134 °C ≥18 min ± NaOH.
Concept: Sterilisation – prions resist autoclaving
Question 10 of 40Pathology High-yield concept
Which electron-microscopic finding indicates IRREVERSIBLE cell injury?
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: D. Large, flocculent, amorphous densities in mitochondria

Explanation

Robbins: the two hallmarks of irreversibility are the inability to restore MITOCHONDRIAL function and severe damage to MEMBRANES. Correct: large flocculent (amorphous) densities in mitochondria, together with nuclear changes (pyknosis, karyorrhexis, karyolysis) and membrane rupture, mean the cell will die. Why the others are wrong: cellular swelling (the earliest change), plasma membrane blebbing, ER dilatation, detachment of ribosomes and myelin figures are all features of REVERSIBLE injury in Robbins.

One-liner to remember: Irreversible = mitochondrial flocculent densities + membrane rupture + nuclear changes; swelling = earliest reversible change.
Concept: Reversible vs irreversible cell injury (EM)
Question 11 of 40Microbiology High-yield concept
A patient presents on day 2 of high fever, headache, retro-orbital pain and myalgia during a dengue outbreak. The MOST appropriate test to confirm dengue now is:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: C. NS1 antigen ELISA

Explanation

Correct: NS1 antigen (or RT-PCR) is positive from day 1 to about day 5 of fever, so it is the test of choice early. Why the others are wrong: IgM becomes detectable only after about day 5, so it can be falsely negative on day 2. Question 12 of 40OBG High-yield concept
Which ovarian tumour is MOST likely to undergo torsion?
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: C. Mature cystic teratoma (dermoid cyst)

Explanation

Correct: the dermoid cyst is the ovarian tumour most prone to torsion (DC Dutta) – it is moderate-sized, has a long pedicle and is heavy because of its fat, hair and teeth, and it is common in young women and pregnancy. Question 13 of 40Orthopedics High-yield concept
A child falls on an outstretched hand. X-ray shows a fracture of the proximal third of the ulna with anterior dislocation of the radial head. The nerve MOST commonly injured is:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: C. Posterior interosseous nerve

Explanation

This is a Monteggia fracture-dislocation (Bado type I, the commonest type, has the radial head dislocated anteriorly). Correct: the posterior interosseous nerve (deep branch of the radial nerve) winds round the radial neck through the supinator and is stretched by the dislocated radial head. The result is finger and thumb drop WITHOUT wrist drop and without sensory loss. Why the others are wrong: the ulnar nerve is classically injured with medial epicondyle fractures. The anterior interosseous nerve is classically injured in supracondylar humerus fractures. The median nerve is not typically injured in Monteggia. Compare GALEAZZI: fracture of the distal radius with dislocation of the distal radio-ulnar joint (DRUJ).

One-liner to remember: Monteggia = ulna fracture + radial head dislocation → PIN palsy; Galeazzi = radius fracture + DRUJ dislocation.
Concept: Monteggia fracture-dislocation – nerve injury
Question 14 of 40Microbiology High-yield concept
A patient has: HBsAg negative, anti-HBs negative, IgM anti-HBc positive. The MOST likely interpretation is:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: B. Window period of acute hepatitis B

Explanation

Correct: in the WINDOW PERIOD, HBsAg has disappeared but anti-HBs has not yet appeared, so IgM anti-HBc is the ONLY marker of acute infection. Why the others are wrong: vaccination produces ONLY anti-HBs (anti-HBc is negative, because the vaccine contains surface antigen only). A chronic carrier is HBsAg-positive for more than 6 months, with IgG anti-HBc. Past infection with recovery shows anti-HBs plus IgG (total) anti-HBc, with HBsAg negative.

One-liner to remember: Only IgM anti-HBc positive = window period; only anti-HBs = vaccinated.

Question 15 of 40Pediatrics High-yield concept
Which ONE of the following types of neonatal jaundice is ALWAYS pathological?
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: A. Clinically visible jaundice appearing within the first 24 hours of life

Explanation

Correct: jaundice in the first 24 hours is ALWAYS pathological – think haemolysis (Rh/ABO incompatibility, G6PD deficiency), sepsis or TORCH; check bilirubin urgently. Why the others are wrong: physiological jaundice appears after 24 h (usually day 2–3) in term babies and is normal. In preterm babies physiological jaundice peaks later (day 5–7) and can last up to about 2 weeks, so day 10 is still acceptable. Breast-milk jaundice (late onset, after day 5–7) can last 3–12 weeks in a thriving baby and is benign – continue breastfeeding, though prolonged jaundice still needs a check for conjugated bilirubin and hypothyroidism.

One-liner to remember: Jaundice <24 h = always pathological (haemolysis first).
Concept: Physiological vs pathological neonatal jaundice
Question 16 of 40Medicine High-yield concept
In a critically ill ICU patient with no thyroid disease, the EARLIEST and most common thyroid function abnormality is:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: C. Low total and free T3 with raised reverse T3

Explanation

In non-thyroidal illness (sick euthyroid syndrome), cytokines and fasting reduce peripheral 5′-deiodinase, so less T4 is converted to T3 and more to reverse T3. Correct: low T3 with raised rT3 is the earliest and commonest change; TSH is usually normal, and in severe illness T4 also falls (a sign of poor prognosis). Why the others are wrong: raised TSH with low T4 is primary hypothyroidism (TSH can rise transiently during RECOVERY, which is a trap). Low TSH with high free T4 is thyrotoxicosis. Reverse T3 RISES, not falls. Do not treat with thyroid hormone.

One-liner to remember: Sick euthyroid: ↓T3, ↑rT3, normal TSH; ↓T4 = severe illness/poor prognosis.
Concept: Sick euthyroid (non-thyroidal illness) syndrome
Question 17 of 40Pediatrics High-yield concept
A 9-month-old with tetralogy of Fallot becomes deeply cyanosed, irritable and hyperpnoeic, and the murmur becomes softer. Which drug is CONTRAINDICATED in this situation?
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: A. Isoprenaline (isoproterenol) infusion

Explanation

This is a hypercyanotic (Tet) spell: infundibular spasm increases right-to-left shunt, so less blood crosses the pulmonary valve and the murmur SOFTENS. Correct: inotropes such as isoprenaline (and digoxin) increase contractility, worsen infundibular spasm and deepen cyanosis – they are contraindicated. Why the others are useful: phenylephrine raises systemic vascular resistance, pushing blood into the lungs. Morphine reduces hyperpnoea and catecholamine drive. Propranolol relaxes the infundibulum and slows the heart. Also used: knee-chest position, oxygen and sodium bicarbonate for acidosis.

One-liner to remember: Tet spell: knee-chest, O₂, morphine, propranolol, phenylephrine, NaHCO₃ – NO inotropes; murmur softens during spell.
Concept: Tetralogy of Fallot – hypercyanotic (Tet) spell

Question 19 of 40Pharmacology High-yield concept
A 7-year-old girl has frequent staring spells lasting a few seconds; the EEG shows 3 Hz spike-and-wave discharges and she has no other seizure types. The drug of choice is:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: A. Ethosuximide

Explanation

This is childhood absence epilepsy. Correct: ethosuximide (a T-type Ca²⁺ channel blocker in thalamic neurons) is the drug of choice for PURE absence seizures – as effective as valproate with fewer side effects and without valproate's teratogenic and metabolic risks, which matters for a girl. Why the others are wrong: carbamazepine and phenytoin can WORSEN absence (and myoclonic) seizures. Valproate works, but is preferred when absence occurs WITH generalised tonic-clonic or myoclonic seizures, and it is avoided where possible in girls because of teratogenicity, weight gain and PCOS.

One-liner to remember: Pure absence: ethosuximide; absence + GTCS: valproate; carbamazepine/phenytoin worsen absence.
Concept: Absence seizures – drug of choice

Question 21 of 40Psychiatry High-yield concept
Which clinical feature BEST favours serotonin syndrome over neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS)?
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: A. Inducible clonus and hyperreflexia, with onset within 24 hours

Explanation

Correct: serotonin syndrome (from SSRIs, MAOIs, linezolid, tramadol, triptans) begins quickly, within hours to 24 h, with neuromuscular HYPERactivity – clonus (especially of the lower limbs), hyperreflexia, myoclonus, mydriasis and diarrhoea. Question 22 of 40PSM / Community Medicine High-yield concept
Under India's open vial policy, which vaccine vial, once opened, can be used in subsequent sessions for up to 4 weeks if all conditions are met?
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: A. Oral polio vaccine (OPV)

Explanation

Correct: the open vial policy applies to LIQUID vaccines – OPV, DPT, Td/TT, Hepatitis B, Pentavalent, PCV and IPV. An opened vial can be reused for up to 4 weeks (28 days) if it is within its expiry date, kept in the cold chain, the VVM has not reached the discard point, it is not contaminated, and the date and time of opening are written on it. Children with nephrotic syndrome lose immunoglobulins (IgG) and factor B in urine, which reduces opsonisation of encapsulated bacteria. ✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: D. Treatment is in-situ fixation with a single cannulated screw, without forceful reduction

Explanation

The image shows a slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE): the epiphysis has slipped posteriorly and medially off the metaphysis. Knee pain is referred via the obturator nerve – always examine the hip in a child with knee pain. Correct: pin IN SITU with a single cannulated screw; forced reduction risks avascular necrosis. Why the others are wrong: on hip flexion the thigh goes into obligatory EXTERNAL rotation (Drehmann sign). Klein's line along the superior neck cuts LESS of the epiphysis, or misses it completely (Trethowan sign). SCFE typically affects obese adolescent BOYS aged 10–16; age 4–8 years fits Perthes disease.

One-liner to remember: SCFE: obese adolescent boy, knee pain, obligatory external rotation, Trethowan sign; pin in situ.

Question 30 of 40Physiology High-yield concept
Which hormone acts through a receptor linked to the JAK–STAT pathway?
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: A. Growth hormone

Explanation

Correct: growth hormone acts through a cytokine-type receptor with no intrinsic kinase activity; it recruits JAK2 tyrosine kinase, which phosphorylates STAT proteins. Prolactin, erythropoietin, leptin, G-CSF and the interleukins work the same way. Why the others are wrong: the insulin receptor (like IGF-1, EGF and PDGF receptors) is a receptor tyrosine kinase with its OWN intrinsic kinase. Glucagon acts through a Gs-coupled receptor that raises cAMP. Aldosterone is a steroid acting on an intracellular (nuclear) receptor.

One-liner to remember: JAK-STAT: GH, prolactin, EPO, leptin, cytokines; intrinsic tyrosine kinase: insulin, IGF-1, growth factors.
Concept: Hormone receptor signalling – JAK-STAT

A 28-year-old has knee pain. X-ray shows an eccentric, lytic, expansile 'soap-bubble' lesion in the distal femoral epiphysis extending to the subchondral bone, with no matrix calcification. The physis is fused. Most likely diagnosis:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: B. Giant cell tumour (osteoclastoma)

Explanation

Location plus skeletal maturity is the key. Correct: giant cell tumour arises in the EPIPHYSIS (extending to the metaphysis and subchondral bone) AFTER the growth plate has FUSED, typically at age 20–40. It is eccentric and lytic with a soap-bubble look, and is locally aggressive. Why the others are wrong: chondroblastoma is also epiphyseal but occurs BEFORE physeal closure (skeletally immature, age 10–20) and often shows chondroid calcification. A patient with bipolar disorder has had 5 mood episodes (mania and depression) in the past year. The mood stabiliser of choice is:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: A. Sodium valproate

Explanation

Four or more mood episodes in 12 months = RAPID CYCLING. Correct: valproate (and other anticonvulsant mood stabilisers such as lamotrigine) works better than lithium in rapid cycling. Why the others are wrong: lithium is the classic mood stabiliser but responds POORLY in rapid cycling (it works best in classic euphoric mania). Question 33 of 40Medicine High-yield concept
A 40-year-old has BP 170/100 mmHg, serum K⁺ 2.9 mEq/L, metabolic alkalosis and urine chloride 45 mEq/L. The most likely cause is:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: D. Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn syndrome)

Explanation

First split metabolic alkalosis by URINE CHLORIDE. Below about 20 mEq/L it is saline-responsive (vomiting, nasogastric suction, past diuretic use, post-hypercapnia). Above 20 mEq/L it is saline-resistant – then look at the BP. Correct: saline-resistant + HYPERTENSION = mineralocorticoid excess (Conn syndrome, Cushing, Liddle, renal artery stenosis). Why the others are wrong: vomiting causes a LOW urine chloride (<10–20). Bartter (and Gitelman) are saline-resistant but NORMOTENSIVE. Post-hypercapnic alkalosis is chloride-responsive.

One-liner to remember: Metabolic alkalosis: urine Cl <20 = vomiting (saline-responsive); Cl >20 + high BP = Conn/Liddle; Cl >20 + normal BP = Bartter/Gitelman/diuretics.

Question 34 of 40Orthopedics High-yield concept
A young man with a closed tibial shaft fracture in an above-knee cast complains of increasing pain despite opioids. The EARLIEST and most reliable sign of acute compartment syndrome is:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: D. Pain on passive stretching of the muscles of the compartment

Explanation

Correct: pain out of proportion to the injury, especially pain on PASSIVE STRETCH of the involved muscles, is the earliest and most sensitive sign. Why the others are wrong: compartment pressure rarely exceeds ARTERIAL pressure, so distal pulses usually stay PRESENT, and waiting for them to disappear is a dangerous trap. Pallor, pulselessness and paralysis are LATE signs, appearing when muscle or nerve damage is already happening. Management: split or remove the cast down to the skin; if the pressure difference (diastolic BP minus compartment pressure) is under 30 mmHg, do an urgent fasciotomy.

One-liner to remember: Compartment syndrome: earliest = pain on passive stretch; pulses usually present; ΔP <30 mmHg → fasciotomy.
Concept: Acute compartment syndrome – earliest sign
Question 35 of 40OBG High-yield concept
As per the Government of India (DIPSI) guideline, gestational diabetes is diagnosed by:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: C. 75 g oral glucose irrespective of the last meal; 2-hour plasma glucose ≥140 mg/dL

Explanation

Correct: the DIPSI 'single-step' test – 75 g oral glucose given WITHOUT regard to fasting or last meal, with a 2-hour venous plasma glucose ≥140 mg/dL diagnosing GDM. It is done at the first antenatal visit and repeated at 24–28 weeks if negative. Why the others are wrong: the 100 g 3-hour OGTT (Carpenter–Coustan / NDDG) is the second step of the US two-step method. The 50 g challenge test is only a SCREEN – a value ≥140 mg/dL needs a confirmatory OGTT. A 2-hour value ≥200 mg/dL is the cut-off for overt diabetes, not GDM.

One-liner to remember: DIPSI: 75 g, non-fasting, 2-h plasma glucose ≥140 mg/dL = GDM.
Concept: GDM screening – DIPSI (Govt of India)

IMAGE-BASED: A 24-year-old with recurrent palpitations has this resting ECG. He later presents with an irregular, broad-complex tachycardia at about 240/min (pre-excited AF) but is haemodynamically stable. Which drug is CONTRAINDICATED?
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: D. IV verapamil

Explanation

The ECG shows a short PR interval with slurred upstrokes of the QRS (delta waves) – Wolff–Parkinson–White pattern. In pre-excited AF, blocking the AV node pushes more impulses down the accessory pathway, which can cause ventricular fibrillation. Correct: AV-nodal blockers – verapamil, diltiazem, digoxin, adenosine and β-blockers – are contraindicated. Why the others are wrong: procainamide or ibutilide slow conduction in the accessory pathway and are the drugs of choice when the patient is stable. One-liner to remember: Pre-excited AF in WPW: procainamide/ibutilide or DC shock; NEVER AV-nodal blockers (verapamil, digoxin, adenosine).

Question 37 of 40PSM / Community Medicine High-yield concept
A screening test with fixed sensitivity and specificity is used in a population where the prevalence of the disease INCREASES. What happens?
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: C. PPV increases; sensitivity and specificity remain unchanged

Explanation

Correct: sensitivity and specificity are properties of the TEST and do not change with prevalence. Predictive values depend on prevalence: as prevalence rises, the positive predictive value RISES (more of the positives are true positives) and the negative predictive value FALLS. Why the others are wrong: sensitivity and specificity do not change with prevalence (they change only if the cut-off is changed). NPV moves in the OPPOSITE direction, so it decreases.

One-liner to remember: ↑Prevalence → ↑PPV, ↓NPV; sensitivity and specificity unchanged.

Five days after subarachnoid haemorrhage, a patient has serum Na⁺ 126 mEq/L, high urine sodium, postural hypotension, a rising haematocrit and weight loss. The most appropriate management is:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: D. Volume and sodium replacement with isotonic/hypertonic saline (± fludrocortisone)

Explanation

Both SIADH and cerebral salt wasting (CSW) cause hyponatraemia with a high urine Na⁺; the KEY difference is volume status. Here there are signs of HYPOVOLAEMIA (postural drop, high haematocrit, weight loss), so this is CSW. Correct: replace salt and volume (0.9% or 3% saline, ± fludrocortisone). An 8-month-old infant has severe dehydration from acute diarrhoea and IV access is obtained. As per WHO Plan C, Ringer lactate should be given as:
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: B. 30 mL/kg in 1 hour, then 70 mL/kg in the next 5 hours

Explanation

WHO Plan C for severe dehydration uses 100 mL/kg Ringer lactate (or normal saline) in two parts, and the SPEED depends on age. Correct: infants UNDER 12 months get 30 mL/kg in 1 hour, then 70 mL/kg in 5 hours (total 6 h). Question 40 of 40Psychiatry High-yield concept
A patient stable on lithium develops coarse tremor, vomiting and ataxia shortly after a new drug is added. Which drug is the MOST likely cause?
✓ Correct!
✗ Incorrect. Correct answer: B. Hydrochlorothiazide

Explanation

Lithium is handled like sodium in the proximal tubule. Correct: thiazides cause mild sodium depletion, so the proximal tubule reabsorbs more sodium and lithium, raising lithium levels by 25–40% and causing toxicity. ACE inhibitors, ARBs and most NSAIDs do the same. Why the others are wrong: theophylline, acetazolamide (a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor) and osmotic diuretics such as mannitol INCREASE lithium excretion and LOWER its levels. Coarse tremor, vomiting, ataxia and confusion suggest toxicity (usually >1.5 mEq/L); fine tremor is a common side effect at therapeutic levels.

One-liner to remember: Lithium ↑ by thiazides, ACEi/ARB, NSAIDs; ↓ by theophylline, acetazolamide, osmotic diuretics.
Concept: Lithium – drug interactions raising levels
